They Called Him The Church Treasurer

On a Tuesday morning, Dolores walked into her bank and was told her account had been closed nine months earlier. She had been depositing into it every single Friday.

The teller turned the monitor toward her and pointed to a line dated the previous autumn. The balance was zero. Dolores had kept every receipt in a blue envelope in the kitchen drawer, and the receipts showed a balance of forty one thousand, eight hundred and sixteen dollars.

Dolores called Harold from the bank lobby. Harold had been the church treasurer for nine years, the man who counted the Sunday collection after everyone else had gone home.

Before Harold arrived, Dolores placed the blue envelope on the counter. The teller compared deposits with the bank record. The amounts were close, but the account numbers were not the same. Dolores had been handing her money to Harold at the church office.

Nine years earlier, Harold was the person people asked to carry the keys. He was fifty eight, worked mornings at a heating company, and arrived at the church before the caretaker. He knew which cupboard held the spare candles and which drawer held the old donation books.

His wife had died in late winter, and after that he began staying after the Wednesday service to finish the accounts. Dolores was the first to bring him a question about the church reserve. She wanted to set aside money for the roof repairs, but she did not want the cash sitting in her house.

Harold offered a simple arrangement. He would record each payment in the reserve ledger, place the cash in the church account, and give Dolores a receipt from the office book. The church already had a bank relationship, and the process looked familiar. Dolores gave him two hundred and forty dollars that Friday.

Within a month, six people were making the same deposits. Harold kept a separate page for each person, using their names, dates, and amounts. The pages were kept behind the regular ledger, because the reserve was not part of the Sunday collection and Harold said the books would be easier to reconcile that way.

In the third year, Harold asked Dolores to make deposits into a new account number. He said the church had changed banks and the old slips would no longer scan. The new slips were green instead of white. They carried the church name, but the account number was printed in a smaller type.

Dolores noticed that the new account number had twelve characters and the old one had ten. She mentioned it to Harold in the corridor. He looked at the slip, tapped the paper with his finger, and said the bank had changed its numbering system.

The new account accepted deposits for almost four years. Harold moved money between three accounts, using transfers that appeared to come from the church reserve. He used new deposits to cover withdrawals from older pages, then entered the transfers in a workbook that never went to the church council.

The system held because no one asked for all the pages at once. Each depositor knew his or her own total. The council saw a clean summary. The bank saw ordinary transfers between accounts. Harold saw the gap growing by a few hundred dollars at a time.

In the ninth year, the roof repair was finally approved. The contractor asked for a deposit of seventeen thousand, nine hundred and sixty dollars. Dolores offered to release part of her reserve. Harold said the account had already been moved and that the bank would send the money directly.

The money did not arrive. Harold said the transfer had been held for review, then said the contractor had changed the invoice. During those three weeks, Martin asked for his money to pay a medical bill. Harold gave him a cheque from the church account.

The cheque cleared, but it was followed by two more withdrawals. The older depositors were paid from money that had arrived from newer depositors. Harold did not need a new story each time. He needed only another Friday and one more receipt.

Dolores went to the bank after the teller called her about a returned transfer. The teller searched by her name and found no personal reserve account. She searched by the church name and found an account that had been closed nine months before. The bank had sent the closing notice to an address Harold had supplied.

Harold arrived at the branch wearing the same dark coat he wore for council meetings. He said the bank had made an error. The teller asked for the original deposit slips. Dolores placed them on the counter, one by one, until the blue envelope was empty.

The slips carried a routing line that pointed to a real bank, but the account belonged to a small holding company that Harold had registered after his wife died. The company had no office and no employees. Its only regular activity was receiving the church deposits and sending money back when someone requested a withdrawal.

The total shortfall was two hundred and eighteen thousand, six hundred and forty two dollars. Forty seven people had deposited over nine years. Twelve had received nearly everything they put in. Twenty nine had lost between two thousand and nine thousand dollars. Six people had lost more than ten thousand.

Harold admitted the transfers in a statement written over two afternoons. He said the first withdrawal had paid for a furnace repair after his wife died. He said he planned to replace the money before anyone noticed.

That was the machine. Harold took a deposit, recorded it on a page that looked official, and placed the money where it could serve two records. One record promised a balance to a person. The other record showed a movement of money to a bank. Neither record showed the whole path.

Three details had been visible. The account numbers changed without a new agreement. The council received summaries instead of statements. The receipts were always available, but the supporting bank records were not.

Harold had also made the scheme harder to see by keeping the amounts ordinary. Most deposits were below five hundred dollars. Most withdrawals were below three thousand. The numbers did not create a single dramatic event. They created a habit.

Dolores kept the blue envelope in the same kitchen drawer, but she no longer used it for receipts. The last paper inside it was the bank notice dated nine months before she opened it.
