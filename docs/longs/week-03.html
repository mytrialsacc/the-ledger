The Roof Was Never Fixed

Script copy karo

The county has a file on this man that is four inches thick. It begins with a roof that was never fixed.

Leon Mercer stood in a courtroom in November of two thousand twenty three and listened while a clerk read the number eighty four thousand, two hundred and nineteen dollars. That was the amount homeowners had paid for work that did not exist. Leon had collected deposits from nineteen houses. Seven roofs were opened. None were finished.

The file contained photographs of blue tarps, bare plywood, and rooms where rain had reached the ceiling. It contained copies of permits, invoices, cancelled cheques, and handwritten notes from people who had slept in their living rooms for weeks. The county called it pattern. Leon called it a run of bad weather and delayed supplies.

Grace Nolan was the first homeowner to speak. She had paid Leon twelve thousand, four hundred and eighty dollars in March of two thousand nineteen. Leon removed three rows of shingles, placed a tarp over the opening, and did not return for twenty eight days. The tarp came loose on the ninth night.

Grace kept a towel beneath the ceiling fan. She moved her dining table against the wall because water had started falling beside the light fixture. Leon sent messages saying the supplier had changed its delivery date. He sent a photograph of a truck bed carrying bundles of shingles. The bundles were not Grace's.

The second house belonged to Marcus and Helen, aged seventy two. They paid seventeen thousand, six hundred dollars for a full roof and new gutters. Leon took half on the first day. He took the other half after showing them a permit with their address printed across the top.

The permit was real. It had been issued by the county for a different contractor who had withdrawn from the job two months earlier. Leon had copied the permit number onto his own invoice. The county system showed a valid record, but it did not show the name of the person standing in Marcus and Helen's driveway.

By the time the county began calling homeowners, Leon had used five business names. The names appeared on estimates, truck magnets, and envelopes left in kitchens. None of the businesses had a staff list. None had a permanent office.

The work made the invoices look more believable. Leon measured a roof, counted the vents, and noticed the age of the gutters. He knew the language of repair. He could tell Grace that the underlayment was soft without touching it. He could explain a flashing problem while standing in the shade of a porch.

Leon had learned the trade from an uncle in another county. He knew how long a real roof should take and which parts of a job were hard to see from the ground. He also knew that homeowners felt relief when somebody arrived with a ladder and a clipboard. The first visit did not look like a sale.

In July of two thousand eighteen, Leon began leaving cards in neighborhoods after heavy storms. The cards had no phone number printed on them. They had a name, a county license number, and a promise to inspect damage without charge. The license number belonged to a roofer who had closed his business the year before.

The first homeowner to call was Martha Bell. A tree limb had cracked two rafters above her back room. Leon arrived on a Tuesday afternoon in a white van with a ladder tied to the roof. He walked around the house twice before he knocked.

Martha was sixty six and had lived in the house since her children were small. She showed Leon the water mark near the window. Leon climbed onto the roof, stayed there for nine minutes, and came down carrying a piece of broken shingle. He said the damage was larger than the mark suggested.

He wrote an estimate on the hood of his van. The total was nine thousand, eight hundred and forty dollars. He said the first payment would cover materials and put her on the crew schedule. Martha asked when the work could begin. Leon said the following Monday.

On Monday, Leon arrived with one labourer and removed the damaged shingles. They worked for three hours. Before leaving, Leon placed a blue tarp over the open section and said the materials truck would arrive in two days. The truck did not arrive.

Martha called the number on the estimate. Leon answered on the first call and said the supplier had sent the wrong bundles. On the second call, he said the county inspector had changed the order of the jobs. On the third call, he said the labourer had injured his hand.

Martha did not know that the first deposit had already been divided. Three thousand dollars went to a storage unit. Two thousand went to a credit card balance. The rest paid for fuel, groceries, and a deposit on the next job. The project in Martha's back room had become a source of cash for a different roof.

For five years, the same arrangement moved through nearby streets. Leon arrived quickly after storms. He made the first repair look real. He collected enough money to begin the next house. When a homeowner complained, he sent a photograph from another roof and gave a new date.

The first signal was not hidden. Leon's license number belonged to somebody else. The second was visible on every estimate. The company address was a mailbox. The third appeared in the payment schedule. The largest amount was due before the first bundle of materials had reached the driveway.

The county file grew because each homeowner carried only one part of it. Grace had the tarp photographs. Marcus and Helen had the permit copy. Martha had the first estimate and the dates of three missed visits. The pattern became clear when the documents were placed in one room.

Leon never built a roof that could hold the story together. He built a sequence of first days. Each first day had a ladder, a measurement, a partial repair, and a reason to return. The deposits kept the sequence moving until the county stopped issuing permits in his names.

The first time Leon entered a neighborhood, he wore a clean shirt and carried a clipboard. Martha opened the front door because a tree had damaged the roof and somebody had arrived before dark. Leon stood in the driveway, looked up at the broken rafters, and offered to take a closer look. Martha shook Leon's hand and gave him the key.
